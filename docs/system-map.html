PIRRASYSTEM MAP v0.1WORKING ARCHITECTUREAUG 2026

The paralegal runs it. The attorney touches it twice.

Attorney authorizes → PIRRA captures → paralegal runs → attorney adjudicates → exhibits out. One text thread for the client, three severable stores underneath, and a hard boundary around what PIRRA is allowed to talk about.

WHY THIS MAP

What it commits us to

Three decisions from this week are now load-bearing, and each one shows up as structure rather than policy. The record is a produce-expected document, so it is stored apart from everything that must never be produced. The attorney is genuinely in the thread, which upgrades the privilege theory from Kovel-agent to ordinary conduit — but only if the attorney's presence is real and logged. And PIRRA is a damages instrument, not a liability instrument, which turns out to be a containment problem rather than a topic preference.

ACTORS

Four roles, and what each one cannot do

The negative column matters more than the positive one. Every constraint below is doing legal work, not product-scoping work.

ATTORNEY

Authorizes the conversation protocol at case open. Adjudicates evidentiary flags. Approves exhibits before they leave. Replies in-thread when the client needs counsel.

Cannot delegate protocol authorization or evidentiary-flag disposition. Both are credential-locked.

PARALEGAL

Primary operator. Works the queue, clears operational flags, calls the client when PIRRA surfaces a gap, assembles exhibits, watches the digest.

Cannot dispose of an evidentiary flag, or send a message that reads as legal advice.

PIRRA

Texts the client on the firm's number. Captures present-condition statements. Classifies and flags. Always discloses it is an AI provided by the firm.

Cannot discuss liability, advise, evaluate the case, or pursue a topic the protocol did not authorize.

CLIENT

Texts back. No app, no login, no download. Sees one thread with three plainly labeled speakers, and is told at intake where the record can end up.

Cannot delete messages once under representation — archival only, never true deletion.

FIGURE 1 — LIFECYCLE

Where each role touches the case

The attorney appears at exactly two points: authorizing the protocol at the top, and adjudicating evidentiary flags in the middle. Everything between is the paralegal's. That is what keeps attorney-directed true without making it expensive.

FIGURE 2 — STORAGE

One thread the client sees, three stores that must come apart

This is the architectural claim the whole legal position rests on. The client experiences a single continuous text thread — that is the product. Underneath, the messages land in three separate stores with three different discovery postures, and exactly one of them has a path out of the building.

If these ever share a table with a visibility flag on each row, one bad export destroys the work-product claim for that firm and hands the defense an impeachment file on their own client. Severability is not a feature to add later; it is the schema.

FIGURE 3 — SCOPE

The liability boundary

PIRRA talks about damages. It does not talk about how the injury happened, who was at fault, how fast anyone was going, or what anyone admitted at the scene. That boundary is not squeamishness — it follows directly from Stream 1 being produce-expected.

A client's contemporaneous statement about causation is the single most dangerous artifact you can manufacture in a personal-injury case, and it goes straight to the defense. "I only looked down for a second" ends the case, and it ends it in a hash-chained, independently timestamped, self-authenticating record that the firm built on purpose. Liability facts already live where they belong — the police report, intake, the deposition — all attorney-controlled.

THE NON-OBVIOUS CONSEQUENCE

Because the log is immutable, a liability statement that arrives cannot be removed — deleting it is spoliation, and doing so inside a tamper-evident chain makes the deletion provable. So the only real control is upstream, in the prompt library.

Which means one specific rule: PIRRA never opens with, or returns to, how the injury happened. That is the most natural rapport move in human conversation and it must be designed out of the protocol entirely. The onboarding disclosure states the boundary in plain language so the client knows where to put it — "anything about how it happened, save that for your legal team."

AUTHORITY

Who can dispose of what

The split is not about seniority. An operational flag asks someone to make a phone call; an evidentiary flag asks someone to make a judgment about the case. The second is legal work, and the attorney's disposition of it is also what puts mental impressions into Stream 2 — which is what makes it opinion work product rather than ordinary work product.

FlagTriggerDispositionWhy
Missed appointment gap in treatment cadence PARALEGAL Operational. Call the client, log the contact, close it.
Client gone quiet non-response window PARALEGAL Operational. Logged with a neutral status code — silence never reads as recovery.
New symptom unreported complaint PARALEGAL Operational, but routes to the attorney if it suggests a new injury claim.
Account inconsistency statement conflicts with record ATTORNEY Legal judgment about credibility. A paralegal dismissing this is a Rule 5.3 problem.
Adverse statement content that damages the claim ATTORNEY Requires case strategy, not a callback.
Liability volunteered causation content detected ATTORNEY Immediate. Quarantined, undeletable, and now in a produce-expected stream.
TAMPER-EVIDENCE CUTS BOTH WAYS

An unacknowledged flag sitting for sixty days inside a hash-chained, independently timestamped record is proof the firm was on notice and did nothing. Handled well that is a selling point — a documented trail that the firm saw it and acted. Handled badly it is the exhibit that gets PIRRA sued alongside its customer.

So the acknowledgment workflow needs an SLA, escalation when it lapses, and a real conversation with outside counsel about flag retention before the pilot.

BUILD TARGET

The paralegal workbench

Five surfaces. The attorney gets the same application with two extra permissions and a digest — not a separate product, and not a separate login to forget about.

  1. Queue

    Every active case, sorted by what needs attention rather than alphabetically. Unacknowledged flags first, then quiet clients, then everything healthy. This is the screen that is open all day.

  2. Case view

    The timeline built from the client's own words, with each entry tagged by hearsay posture and clicking through to the exact message. Flags in a rail alongside. Attorney-only flags visible but locked.

  3. Thread

    The full conversation, three speakers plainly labeled. The paralegal can send as themselves; requesting an attorney reply is a distinct action that routes to the attorney rather than letting anyone speak as counsel.

  4. Flag disposition

    Acknowledge, act, and note. Operational flags close here. Evidentiary flags are read-only for the paralegal and carry a visible SLA clock, because the clock is a legal artifact.

  5. Exhibit builder

    Select a range, pick an output — demand-packet exhibit, recovery-curve chart, depo-prep log, prior-consistent-statement packet — and every figure carries its source reference. Draws only from Stream 1, structurally. Attorney approval gates the export.

STILL OPEN

What this map does not yet answer

  • Case closure. Settlement lands — then what? Retention policy, export to the firm file, the client's own copy, and the billing event all need a decision. Immutability governs during representation; after is unwritten.
  • Attorney presence, proven. The conduit theory depends on the attorney genuinely being in the thread. We need a real answer to "how often did you actually read this?" — which means a minimum touch cadence and a way to show it.
  • Practice-management sync. Without it, PIRRA is the dashboard the pitch explicitly promises not to be. Which streams sync, and how does Stream 2 stay severable once it lands in Clio or Filevine?
  • Unit economics under load. The $149 flat price assumes light usage, but the best cases are the most engaged ones. Model the deeply-engaged client, not the average one.
  • Rule 1.4 expectations. A client texting "should I take the offer?" on a Friday night now has a channel they believe counsel is reading. Response-time expectations need to be set explicitly at onboarding.
